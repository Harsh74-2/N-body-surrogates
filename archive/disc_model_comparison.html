Why the hole? (annulus area)

Bodies are placed at a sampled radius $R$ with uniform azimuth $\varphi$, so the probability of landing in $[R, R+dR]$ is $$p(R)\,dR \;\propto\; R\cdot\Sigma(R)\,dR$$ The $R$ factor is the annulus area $2\pi R\,dR$. The centre has zero area, so the density is zero there regardless of $\Sigma$.

The Plummer sampling PDF peaks at $$R^\star_{\text{Pl}} \;=\; \frac{r_c}{\sqrt{3}}$$ and the Exponential at $$R^\star_{\text{Exp}} \;=\; r_d$$ Increasing $r_c$ makes the Plummer ring bigger. The "donut" you see in the scatter is the geometry, not a bug.

Model differences

Plummer model: 3D spherically symmetric density $\rho \propto (1+r^2/r_c^2)^{-5/2}$; projected surface density $\Sigma_{\text{Pl}}(R) \propto (1+(R/r_c)^2)^{-2}$. It has a finite total mass, a soft core, and stars are more concentrated toward the centre. The sampling probability peaks at $R = r_c/\sqrt{3}$, producing the characteristic ring in the face-on scatter.

Exponential model: Classic thin-disc surface-density profile $\Sigma_{\text{Exp}}(R) \propto e^{-R/r_d}$ with scale length $r_d$. It has infinite total mass if extended to infinity, but we truncate at $r_{\max}$ for normalization. The sampling probability peaks at $R = r_d$, giving a more extended radial distribution than Plummer for the same scale length.

Mixed model: Product $\Sigma_{\text{Mix}}(R) \propto e^{-R/r_d}\,(1+(R/r_c)^2)^{-2.5}$ (the exact form used by the N-body initializer). It combines the concentrated core of the Plummer model with the exponential truncation of the disc model, yielding a realistic galactic disc that declines faster than a pure exponential at large radii due to the extra Plummer factor.

Why the models act this way

Why the hole in the centre? Bodies are dropped uniformly in azimuth, so the probability of landing in [R,R+dR] is p(R)dR∝R⋅Σ(R)dR. The annulus area 2πRdR vanishes at R=0, so even an infinitely peaked Σ cannot put mass in the centre. This is geometry, not a bug — galaxies look like donuts when sampled this way.

Why does Plummer collapse fastest? Plummer packs more mass into small radii, so the central potential well is deep and steep. Equal-mass Plummer bodies feel strong mutual attraction, and the disc is geometrically dense near the core — small perturbations from initial-condition noise grow into radial inflow, so the ring tightens and the inner spike sharpens. The integrator stays symplectic ( ΔE/E0∼10-3 ), so this is real gravitational evolution, not numerical drift.

Why does the Exponential stay smooth? The exponential profile places less mass per unit radius at small R than Plummer (no core concentration), and M(<R)∝1-e-R/rd saturates gently. The self-gravitational pull at any radius is therefore weaker, and circular-orbit initial conditions are closer to true equilibrium. The result is a disc that rotates almost rigidly with little radial rearrangement — exactly what real spiral-galaxy discs do on short timescales.

Why does the Mixed model look "in between"? At small R the Plummer factor (1+(R/rc)2)-5/2 dominates and concentrates mass like a Plummer sphere. At large R the exponential factor e-R/rd dominates and truncates faster than either pure profile would. So the Mixed disc inherits Plummer's tight core and the Exponential's clean edge, which is why it is the profile the N-body initializer in simulation_3d.py actually uses.

Why does the exponential tail stretch further? Look at vφ(R)=G⋅M(<R)/R. Plummer's M(<R) saturates quickly because the (1+(R/rc)2)-2 factor crushes the integrand at large R. The exponential, by contrast, contributes a long, slowly-decreasing tail, so M(<R) keeps growing further out — pushing the velocity curve higher and letting bodies orbit at larger radii.

Why the sampling ring R⋆=rc/3? The peak of p(R)∝R⋅(1+(R/rc)2)-2 is found by d/dR[(1+x2)-2+2x(1+x2)-3⋅x/2]=0 with x=R/rc, giving x2=1/3, so R⋆=rc/3. For the exponential the analogous maximisation gives R⋆=rd exactly. The Mixed profile has no closed-form peak; the code finds it numerically.

Why are the orbits so different on screen? A Plummer body at R⋆=rc/3 has a small radius and a high circular speed, so it sweeps through many orbits per second. An exponential body at R⋆=rd has a larger radius and a lower speed (longer period), so it appears to drift. The video is real time — they are not running on different clocks.

What you should see

  • Drag $r_c$: the orange (Plummer) curve fattens/thins; bodies in the scatter and the histogram redistribute; the KS test re-evaluates.
  • Drag $r_d$: the green (Exponential) curve and the violet (Mixed) curve both stretch; the exponential tail now extends further.
  • Drag $r_{\max}$: this is the hard cutoff used by the sampler. Nothing past it on the $x$-axis of any chart.
  • Drag $N$: more bodies, smoother histograms, smaller $D_N$ (the KS statistic scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$).
  • Drag $\sigma_z$: only the edge-on $(x,z)$ scatter changes. Other charts are 2D-projected, so $\sigma_z$ doesn't affect them — by design.
  • Resample: advances the seed. The numeric seed in the status bar changes; bodies shuffle; the URL hash updates; the N-body video resets to $t=0$.
  • Copy link: copies a URL whose fragment encodes every parameter and seed. Opening that URL in another tab reproduces the view exactly.
