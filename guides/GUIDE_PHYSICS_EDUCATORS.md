# For Physics Educators

Classroom-ready demonstrations of orbital mechanics, energy conservation, and
chaotic drift — generated by an N-body pipeline you can inspect line by line.

## What you can show in class tomorrow

- **Kepler's third law, checked numerically.** The Solar-System presets are
  real: initial conditions come from the NASA JPL Horizons ephemeris, and the
  leapfrog reference integrator reproduces the orbits the textbook predicts.
- **Energy conservation as a plot, not a slogan.** The symplectic leapfrog
  (Störmer–Verlet) integrator keeps energy drift bounded — the reference
  curves in the stability plots show what "conservative" looks like over a
  128-step rollout.
- **Error growth made visible.** The side-by-side animations show the
  reference orbit and the neural surrogate on the same axes: students can
  *see* a small per-step error turn into a visible orbital drift.
- **Scale and mass ratio.** The `solar_system_extended` preset mixes
  heliocentric orbits with Galilean satellites (mass ratios ~10^5). Watch how
  every model's error grows when two length scales coexist — an honest,
  intuitive lesson in why multi-scale systems are hard.

## Suggested classroom sequence

1. Show the reference-only animation for `sun_earth_only`. Ask: what does
   "the orbit closes" tell you about the integrator?
2. Overlay the surrogate prediction (the same clip, second panel). Have
   students estimate the divergence time themselves before reading the
   published predictive horizon k*.
3. Show `jupiter_galileans` — Io's orbital period vs. Callisto's, in one
   preset — and connect it to the period–distance law.
4. Contrast the disc presets (all bodies similar mass, one plane) with the
   solar-system presets to open a discussion of distribution shift, in
   physics terms: the models learned *this galaxy disc*, not *gravity*.

## The physics that is (and is not) in the models

**In:** Newtonian pairwise gravity, softened by a Plummer potential, integrated
symplectically to generate the training corpus.

**Not in:** relativistic corrections, hydrodynamics, collisions, tides. The
surrogates were trained on rotating discs of roughly equal-mass bodies — they
learn an approximation of the next-state map on that distribution, not a
general theory of gravity. When they track a Keplerian orbit for a while and
then drift, that is distribution shift, and it is the most instructive thing
in the whole project.

## A caution worth teaching

The thesis's central measured result is that **per-step accuracy does not
imply orbital fidelity**: a model can track every planet to ~1% per step and
still be wrong by tens of percent of the system's scale within the rollout
horizon. For any student who will one day trust a learned model in a physical
loop, this is the most important figure in the project.
